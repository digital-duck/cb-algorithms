Payoff

Exam scheduling is not merely a bureaucratic convenience — it is a microcosm of one of the deepest problems in discrete mathematics: assigning scarce resources to competing demands without conflict. Every university runs this computation each semester, yet the instance encountered in practice is NP-hard in general, making it a genuine frontier where theory meets algorithmic engineering. Mastering exam scheduling means mastering the full toolkit assembled in this text: graph coloring, integer linear programming, constraint propagation, and heuristic search.

What exam scheduling achieves. Given a set of examinations $E = \{e_1, \dots, e_n\}$, a set of students $S$, and a finite horizon of time-slots $T = \{1, \dots, k\}$, the core feasibility problem asks for an assignment $\sigma : E \to T$ such that no two examinations sharing at least one enrolled student occupy the same slot. Formally, define the conflict graph $G = (E, \mathcal{C})$ where $\{e_i, e_j\} \in \mathcal{C}$ whenever $|\text{enroll}(e_i) \cap \text{enroll}(e_j)| \geq 1$. A feasible schedule is then precisely a proper $k$-coloring of $G$: no two adjacent vertices share a color (slot). The chromatic number $\chi(G)$ gives the theoretical minimum number of slots required, and computing it is equivalent to solving the scheduling problem optimally.

Connections built throughout this book. Graph coloring (Chapter 3) supplied the conflict-graph model. Integer linear programming (Chapter 5) encoded the coloring constraints as $x_{e,t} \in \{0,1\}$ variables with the system

$$\sum_{t=1}^{k} x_{e,t} = 1 \quad \forall e \in E, \qquad x_{e_i,t} + x_{e_j,t} \leq 1 \quad \forall \{e_i,e_j\} \in \mathcal{C},\; \forall t \in T.$$

Constraint propagation (Chapter 6) prunes the search space before the ILP solver fires, and greedy heuristics (Chapter 4) — ordering vertices by decreasing degree — provide warm-start solutions that cut branch-and-bound time dramatically.

A worked landmark. For the small instance $E = \{A,B,C,D\}$ with conflicts $\{A,B\}, \{B,C\}, \{C,D\}, \{A,D\}, \{A,C\}$, the conflict graph contains an odd cycle $A$-$B$-$C$-$A$, forcing $\chi(G) \geq 3$. A greedy Welsh-Powell coloring assigns slots in three passes and achieves exactly three slots, certifying optimality.

Invitation. The richest single extension of basic feasibility is soft-constraint optimization: minimizing student back-to-back examinations, balancing invigilator load, and respecting room capacities simultaneously. This transforms the coloring problem into a weighted partial MAX-SAT or multi-objective ILP. Chapter 8 develops precisely this generalization — the reader who has followed the argument to this point has every tool needed to engage it fully.
