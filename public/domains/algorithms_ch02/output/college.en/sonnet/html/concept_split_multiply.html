Split Multiply

Definition. Let $R$ be a commutative ring and fix a split base $m \in R$. Every element $a \in R$ admits a split representation

$$a = a_H \cdot m + a_L,$$

where $a_H$ (the high part) and $a_L$ (the low part) are chosen so that $|a_L| < m$ in some absolute-value sense appropriate to $R$. The split multiply of $a$ and $b$ is the computation of the product $a \cdot b$ obtained by expanding via the distributive law and collecting the four partial products:

$$a \cdot b = (a_H m + a_L)(b_H m + b_L) = a_H b_H \cdot m^2 + (a_H b_L + a_L b_H)\cdot m + a_L b_L.$$

This identity holds in any commutative ring, making the operation algebraically universal.

Worked Example. Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $m = 10$, $a = 37$, $b = 52$.

Split: $a_H = 3,\; a_L = 7,\; b_H = 5,\; b_L = 2$.

Compute the four partial products:

| Term | Value | |---|---| | $a_H b_H$ | $3 \cdot 5 = 15$ | | $a_H b_L$ | $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ | | $a_L b_H$ | $7 \cdot 5 = 35$ | | $a_L b_L$ | $7 \cdot 2 = 14$ |

Recombine: $15 \cdot 100 + (6 + 35)\cdot 10 + 14 = 1500 + 410 + 14 = 1924$, which agrees with $37 \times 52 = 1924$. $\square$

Key Theorem (Karatsuba Reduction). The naive split multiply requires four sub-multiplications. Karatsuba (1962) showed that the middle term $a_H b_L + a_L b_H$ can be computed using only one additional multiplication:

$$a_H b_L + a_L b_H = (a_H + a_L)(b_H + b_L) - a_H b_H - a_L b_L.$$

Since $a_H b_H$ and $a_L b_L$ are already known, the total count drops from four multiplications to three, reducing the asymptotic complexity of $n$-digit multiplication from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx O(n^{1.585})$.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

from sympy import symbols, expand

a_H, a_L, b_H, b_L, m = symbols('a_H a_L b_H b_L m')
a = a_H * m + a_L
b = b_H * m + b_L

product = expand(a * b)
karatsuba_mid = (a_H + a_L) * (b_H + b_L) - a_H * b_H - a_L * b_L
product_karatsuba = expand(a_H * b_H * m**2 + karatsuba_mid * m + a_L * b_L)

print("Direct expand:    ", product)
print("Karatsuba expand: ", product_karatsuba)
print("Identical?        ", product == product_karatsuba)  # True

Running this cell confirms symbolically that both expressions are identical, validating that the Karatsuba rearrangement is an exact algebraic identity rather than an approximation.
