Concept Book: Jarniks Algorithm

Payoff

Throughout this chapter, we have built the machinery needed to reason about weighted graphs: adjacency, connectivity, spanning trees, and the cut property. Jarník's algorithm—rediscovered independently by Prim in 1957 and now ubiquitous in applied mathematics—is the natural endpoint of that progression. It shows that a single, local greedy decision, made $n-1$ times in succession, is sufficient to construct a globally optimal structure.

What the algorithm achieves. Given a connected weighted graph $G = (V, E, w)$ with $|V| = n$, Jarník's algorithm maintains a growing set $S \subseteq V$ and, at each step, selects the minimum-weight edge crossing the cut $(S, V \setminus S)$, adding its endpoint to $S$. After $n-1$ iterations the result is a minimum spanning tree $T^*$ satisfying

$$w(T^*) = \min_{T \text{ spanning}} \sum_{e \in T} w(e).$$

The correctness rests on the Cut Property: any minimum-weight edge crossing a cut of $G$ belongs to some minimum spanning tree. Because Jarník's algorithm selects exactly such an edge at every step, each addition is provably safe, and the greedy accumulation yields a global optimum.

Why this is the natural endpoint. Earlier sections established the necessary vocabulary—spanning subgraphs, tree characterisation via $|E| = n-1$, and the exchange argument for forests—precisely so that the Cut Property could be stated and proved without hand-waving. The algorithm is therefore not a trick but an inevitable consequence: once you understand what a spanning tree is and why cuts govern edge selection, the greedy strategy writes itself.

Connections to application domains. The reach of minimum spanning trees extends well beyond graph theory. In network design, Jarník's algorithm models the cheapest way to connect cities with cable or pipe, minimising total infrastructure cost. In cluster analysis, removing the $k-1$ heaviest edges of a minimum spanning tree partitions a point cloud into $k$ natural clusters—a technique called single-linkage hierarchical clustering. In image segmentation, pixels become vertices and edge weights encode intensity differences; the minimum spanning tree reveals region boundaries. In approximation algorithms, the Christofides–Serdyuk algorithm uses a minimum spanning tree as its backbone to achieve a $\tfrac{3}{2}$-approximation for the Travelling Salesman Problem.

Each of these domains inherits the same guarantee: the greedy local choice, guided by the Cut Property, produces a structure that is certifiably optimal.
