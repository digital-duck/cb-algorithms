Concept Book: Advanced Maxflow Algorithms

Algorithms Ch11 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Advanced max-flow algorithms are methods for computing a maximum $s$-$t$ flow in a directed capacitated network $G = (V, E, c)$ — where $c : E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ assigns a capacity to each arc — that achieve running times substantially below the $O(VE^2)$ bound of the classical Ford-Fulkerson family. The central examples are Dinic's algorithm, which runs in $O(V^2 E)$ by exploiting level graphs and blocking flows, and the push-relabel algorithm of Goldberg and Tarjan, which achieves $O(V^2 \sqrt{E})$ via local height-function bookkeeping. On unit-capacity graphs, Dinic's algorithm tightens further to $O(E\sqrt{V})$, a bound that is decisive in practice.

Worked example. Consider a grid network with $n = 100$ nodes on each side ($V = 10{,}000$, $E \approx 40{,}000$). Augmenting-path methods require up to $O(VE^2) \approx 6.4 \times 10^{13}$ operations in the worst case. Dinic's algorithm reduces this to $O(V^2 E) \approx 4 \times 10^{12}$, and on this unit-capacity grid the bound $O(E\sqrt{V}) \approx 4 \times 10^6$ is attained. The difference between milliseconds and months of computation is not academic; it is the gap that separates a deployable system from a theoretical curiosity.

Theorem (Max-Flow Min-Cut, Menger 1927 / Ford-Fulkerson 1956). In any network $G$, the value of a maximum $s$-$t$ flow equals the capacity of a minimum $s$-$t$ cut:

$$\max_{f} |f| = \min_{(S,T)} \sum_{(u,v) \in \delta^+(S)} c(u,v).$$

Advanced algorithms do not merely verify this duality — they construct the optimal primal and dual solutions simultaneously, providing a certificate of optimality at no additional cost.

This duality is the thread that ties advanced max-flow to every domain that requires optimal resource allocation under hard constraints: image segmentation (pixels partitioned by minimum cut energy), project scheduling (maximum-weight closure via flow reduction), bipartite matching (König's theorem as a flow corollary), and network interdiction (adversarial minimum-cut selection). Each application is, at bottom, a change of vocabulary applied to the same underlying structure.

Lab cell.

import networkx as nx

G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edge("s", "a", capacity=10.0)
G.add_edge("s", "b", capacity=8.0)
G.add_edge("a", "b", capacity=5.0)
G.add_edge("a", "t", capacity=7.0)
G.add_edge("b", "t", capacity=10.0)

flow_value, flow_dict = nx.maximum_flow(G, "s", "t")
print(f"Maximum flow: {flow_value}")          # 15.0
print(f"Flow on (a,t): {flow_dict['a']['t']}") # 7.0

NetworkX invokes a push-relabel implementation internally; the result confirms the min-cut bound $\min(10+8, 7+10) = 15$. The proof is in the numbers.
