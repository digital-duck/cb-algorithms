What the Minimum Cut Achieves

Every concept introduced in this book — flows, capacities, augmenting paths, residual graphs, and the duality of flows with cuts — converges on a single, elegant question: what is the least we must sever to disconnect a network? The minimum cut problem asks for a partition of the vertex set $V$ of a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ into two disjoint sets $S \ni s$ and $T \ni t$ such that the total capacity of edges crossing from $S$ to $T$,

$$ c(S, T) = \sum_{\substack{u \in S,\, v \in T \\ (u,v)\in E}} c(u, v), $$

is minimised. By the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem, this minimum equals the maximum $s$-$t$ flow — a result that transforms a combinatorial search over exponentially many partitions into a polynomial-time flow computation. Ford and Fulkerson's algorithm, or the $O(VE^2)$ Edmonds–Karp refinement, delivers an exact answer in time that scales gracefully with network size. This is why the minimum cut is the natural endpoint of the theory: it is simultaneously the hardest structural question about a network and the one most completely answered by the machinery we have built.

Worked Example

Consider the six-node supply network below with capacities (in units per hour):

$$ s \xrightarrow{10} a \xrightarrow{9} t, \quad s \xrightarrow{5} b \xrightarrow{6} a, \quad b \xrightarrow{4} t. $$

Running max-flow yields value $14$. The minimum cut is $\{S = \{s, b\},\, T = \{a, t\}\}$, severing edges $(s \to a, 10)$ and $(b \to a, 4)$ for a cut capacity of $14$. No smaller severance can isolate $t$ from $s$. An engineer reading this result knows immediately: reinforce those two edges, and the network's throughput ceiling rises.

Key Theorem

Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem. For any network $(G, c, s, t)$,

$$ \max_f |f| \;=\; \min_{(S,T)} c(S, T). $$

The proof proceeds by showing that when no augmenting path exists in the residual graph $G_f$, the reachable set from $s$ in $G_f$ defines a cut whose capacity equals the current flow value — and no cut can have smaller capacity than any feasible flow.

Lab Cell

import networkx as nx

G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edge('s', 'a', capacity=10)
G.add_edge('s', 'b', capacity=5)
G.add_edge('b', 'a', capacity=4)
G.add_edge('a', 't', capacity=9)
G.add_edge('b', 't', capacity=6)

cut_value, partition = nx.minimum_cut(G, 's', 't')
print(f"Min-cut value: {cut_value}")
print(f"Partition S: {partition[0]}, T: {partition[1]}")

An Invitation

The minimum cut is not merely an abstract optimum — it is a lens through which networks reveal their hidden fragility. In image segmentation, pixels become nodes and intensity gradients become capacities; the minimum cut separates foreground from background in a single pass. In logistics, it locates the supply-chain bottleneck whose removal yields the greatest throughput gain. In social-network analysis, it identifies the smallest set of relationships whose severance isolates one community from another. Each domain reframes the same mathematical object in a different physical language, yet the algorithm and the theorem remain unchanged. Choose one of these arenas — perhaps the one closest to your own field — and ask: where is the cut? The answer will tell you more about the structure of your problem than almost any other single quantity you could compute.
