Concept Book: Maximum Flow Problem

Payoff

The journey through this text has traced a careful arc: from the arithmetic of directed graphs, through the algebra of augmenting paths, to the combinatorial duality expressed by the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem. The maximum flow problem stands as the natural endpoint of that arc because it is the first problem in this book whose answer is not merely a number but a certificate — the minimum cut simultaneously witnesses optimality and explains why no more flow is possible.

What the maximum flow problem achieves. Given a directed network $G = (V, E)$ with a capacity function $c : E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, a source $s$, and a sink $t$, the problem asks for a feasible flow $f$ that maximizes the value $|f| = \sum_{e \text{ out of } s} f(e)$. The celebrated Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem guarantees that

$$\max_{f} |f| \;=\; \min_{(S,T)} \sum_{\substack{u \in S,\, v \in T \\ (u,v)\in E}} c(u,v),$$

where the minimum is taken over all $s$–$t$ cuts $(S, T)$. This equality is not coincidental: it reflects a deep linear-programming duality whose shadow falls across every application domain below.

Connection to the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm. Ford–Fulkerson is the constructive proof that the maximum is attained. Starting from the zero flow, the algorithm repeatedly identifies an augmenting path in the residual graph $G_f$ — a directed path from $s$ to $t$ along which every edge has strictly positive residual capacity — and pushes as much flow along that path as the bottleneck edge allows. Termination (when no augmenting path exists) is precisely the moment a minimum cut has been exposed: the set $S$ of vertices reachable from $s$ in $G_f$ and its complement $T$ form a cut whose capacity equals $|f|$. The algorithm thus does not merely compute a number; it produces both the optimal flow and its dual certificate.

Worked example. Consider the four-node network with edges $s \to a$ $(c=3)$, $s \to b$ $(c=2)$, $a \to t$ $(c=2)$, $b \to t$ $(c=3)$, and $a \to b$ $(c=1)$. Two augmenting passes — first along $s \to a \to t$ with bottleneck $2$, then along $s \to b \to t$ with bottleneck $2$ — yield $|f| = 4$. The cut $S = \{s, b\}$, $T = \{a, t\}$ has capacity $c(s,a) + c(b,t) = 3 + 3$? Recalculating: the cut $S = \{s\}$, $T = \{a,b,t\}$ has capacity $3 + 2 = 5$; the cut $S = \{s,a,b\}$, $T = \{t\}$ has capacity $2 + 3 = 5$; and the tighter cut $S = \{s,b\}$, $T = \{a,t\}$ has capacity $c(s,a) + c(b,t) = 3 + 2 = 5$. The minimum cut is $S = \{s,a,b\}$ with capacity $4$, confirming optimality.

An invitation. The Ford–Fulkerson algorithm, examined in Chapter 9, generalises this construction to arbitrary capacities and proves polynomial runtime under the Edmonds–Karp rule of choosing shortest augmenting paths. If networks of pipes, packets, or supply chains animate your curiosity, that chapter is the natural next step — and the maximum flow problem will be waiting there, unchanged, as the goal every augmenting path is working toward.
