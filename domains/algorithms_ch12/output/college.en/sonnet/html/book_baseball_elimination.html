Payoff

The journey through this text has built, layer by layer, the machinery of network flow: augmenting paths, residual graphs, the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem, and the duality between pushing flow and separating vertices. The problem of baseball elimination is the natural capstone because it is the first place all of these ideas converge on a question that is neither artificially constructed nor trivially answered—it asks, with mathematical precision, whether a team's season is already over before the final game is played.

What baseball elimination achieves. Given a league of $n$ teams, let $w_i$ denote the current wins of team $i$, $r_i$ its remaining games, and $g_{ij}$ the number of remaining games between teams $i$ and $j$. Team $x$ is eliminated if, even assuming it wins all $r_x$ remaining games (finishing with at most $m = w_x + r_x$ total wins), no assignment of outcomes for the remaining inter-team games can place $x$ first. A naïve check would enumerate all $2^{\sum g_{ij}}$ outcome combinations—computationally ruinous for even a modest league. The elegant resolution is a reduction to maximum flow on a carefully constructed network $G = (V, E)$.

Construct $G$ as follows. Introduce a source $s$ and a sink $t$. For each pair $(i, j)$ with $i \neq x$ and $j \neq x$, add a game node $v_{ij}$ with edge $s \to v_{ij}$ of capacity $g_{ij}$, and edges $v_{ij} \to v_i$ and $v_{ij} \to v_j$ each of capacity $\infty$. For each team node $v_i$, add an edge $v_i \to t$ of capacity $m - w_i$. This capacity encodes the maximum additional wins team $i$ may absorb without surpassing $x$.

Theorem (Elimination Criterion). Team $x$ is eliminated if and only if the maximum $s$-$t$ flow in $G$ is strictly less than $\displaystyle\sum_{i < j,\, i,j \neq x} g_{ij}$.

Proof sketch. A feasible flow of value equal to the total games among non-$x$ teams corresponds to a valid outcome assignment; any shortfall, by the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem, corresponds to a cut $(S, T)$ that certifies a subset $S$ of teams collectively guaranteed to exceed $m$ wins regardless of outcomes—the certificate of elimination.

Worked example. Suppose $x$ leads with $m = 90$ projected wins and the remaining three competitors have $g_{AB} = 4$, $w_A = 87$, $w_B = 88$. The source sends $4$ units through $v_{AB}$; team capacities $v_A \to t$ and $v_B \to t$ allow only $3$ and $2$ units respectively. Maximum flow is $4 < 4$ only if constrained—here the cut $\{s, v_{AB}\}$ has capacity $\min(4, 3+2) = 4$, so flow saturates and $x$ survives. Adjust $g_{AB} = 6$: the cap into $t$ is still $5$, flow is $5 < 6$, and $x$ is eliminated. The certificate is the pair $\{A, B\}$, who between them will collectively accumulate at least $87 + 88 + 6 = 181$ wins across two roster slots—averaging above $90$.

# Lab cell — baseball elimination via max-flow (NetworkX)
import networkx as nx

def is_eliminated(w, r, g, x):
    m = w[x] + r[x]
    G = nx.DiGraph()
    G.add_node('s'); G.add_node('t')
    total_games = 0
    teams = [i for i in w if i != x]
    for idx, i in enumerate(teams):
        cap_i = max(0, m - w[i])
        G.add_edge(i, 't', capacity=cap_i)
        for j in teams[idx+1:]:
            gij = g.get((i, j), g.get((j, i), 0))
            total_games += gij
            node = f'g_{i}_{j}'
            G.add_edge('s', node, capacity=gij)
            G.add_edge(node, i, capacity=float('inf'))
            G.add_edge(node, j, capacity=float('inf'))
    flow_val, _ = nx.maximum_flow(G, 's', 't')
    return flow_val < total_games

w = {'A': 87, 'B': 88, 'X': 85}
r = {'A': 3, 'B': 2, 'X': 5}
g = {('A','B'): 6}
print(is_eliminated(w, r, g, 'X'))  # True

Why this is the natural endpoint. Every concept in the preceding chapters earns its keep here. Augmenting paths find the flow; the residual graph certifies when no improvement remains; Min-Cut duality converts a flow value into an interpretable, human-readable witness—a coalition of teams whose collective schedule makes elimination inevitable. The result is not merely computational but explanatory: a sports analyst can point to the certificate and say, precisely, why a team's season is mathematically over.

The same certificate structure—a subset whose aggregate resource demand exceeds capacity—reappears across domains far beyond sport. Supply chains fail when a coalition of suppliers collectively cannot meet aggregate demand. Academic schedules become infeasible when a cluster of courses competes for a bottlenecked room. Electoral coalitions lock in outcomes before all votes are cast. In each case, the network encodes the constraint, flow quantifies the slack, and the min-cut names the obstruction.
