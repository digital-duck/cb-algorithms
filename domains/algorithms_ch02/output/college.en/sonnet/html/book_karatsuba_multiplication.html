Concept Book: Karatsuba Multiplication

Definition. The Karatsuba algorithm is a divide-and-conquer procedure for multiplying two $n$-digit integers in $O(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx O(n^{1.585})$ arithmetic operations, compared to the $O(n^2)$ cost of the classical schoolbook method. Given integers $x$ and $y$, split each at the midpoint $m = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$:

$$x = x_1 \cdot B^m + x_0, \qquad y = y_1 \cdot B^m + y_0,$$

where $B$ is the base. The product expands as

$$xy = x_1 y_1 \cdot B^{2m} + \bigl[(x_1+x_0)(y_1+y_0) - x_1 y_1 - x_0 y_0\bigr] \cdot B^m + x_0 y_0.$$

The key observation is that this requires only three recursive multiplications ($x_1 y_1$, $x_0 y_0$, and $(x_1+x_0)(y_1+y_0)$) rather than four, reducing the recurrence to $T(n) = 3\,T(n/2) + O(n)$, which solves by the Master Theorem to $T(n) = O(n^{\log_2 3})$.

Worked example. Multiply $x = 1234$ and $y = 5678$ in base $10$ with $m = 2$. Set $x_1 = 12,\ x_0 = 34,\ y_1 = 56,\ y_0 = 78$. Compute $z_2 = 12 \times 56 = 672$, $z_0 = 34 \times 78 = 2652$, and $z_1 = (12+34)(56+78) - 672 - 2652 = 46 \times 134 - 3324 = 6164 - 3324 = 2840$. Then

$$1234 \times 5678 = 672 \cdot 10^4 + 2840 \cdot 10^2 + 2652 = 6\,720\,000 + 284\,000 + 2\,652 = 7\,006\,652.$$

Key theorem (Karatsuba–Ofman, 1962). For any $\varepsilon > 0$ and sufficiently large $n$, the Karatsuba algorithm multiplies two $n$-digit integers using at most $3 \cdot n^{\log_2 3}$ single-digit operations, strictly fewer than any classical $O(n^2)$ algorithm for $n > n_0(\varepsilon)$.

This result shattered the long-standing conjecture that $O(n^2)$ was optimal, inaugurating the modern study of arithmetic complexity and establishing the template—recursive splitting plus clever cancellation—that underlies FFT-based multiplication, Strassen matrix products, and polynomial evaluation algorithms throughout computer science and applied mathematics.

from sympy import symbols, expand

x1, x0, y1, y0, B, m = symbols('x1 x0 y1 y0 B m', integer=True)

z2 = x1 * y1
z0 = x0 * y0
z1 = (x1 + x0)*(y1 + y0) - z2 - z0

product = expand(z2*B**(2*m) + z1*B**m + z0)
classical = expand((x1*B**m + x0)*(y1*B**m + y0))

print("Karatsuba == classical:", product == classical)
# Substitute concrete values
vals = {x1: 12, x0: 34, y1: 56, y0: 78, B: 10, m: 2}
print("1234 × 5678 =", product.subs(vals))

The ideas crystallized here—splitting problems at a midpoint, reducing subproblem count through algebraic identities, and bounding cost via a recurrence—appear wherever large-scale computation demands efficiency. The natural next step is to follow Karatsuba's footprint into polynomial multiplication over finite fields, where the same three-multiplication trick underlies the number-theoretic transforms used in modern cryptographic libraries. Spend an afternoon implementing Karatsuba for polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ and you will discover, almost inevitably, why the Fast Fourier Transform felt like the only possible next move.
